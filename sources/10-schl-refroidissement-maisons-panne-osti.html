Cooling rates of houses during extended power failures

Abstract

The 1998 ice storm in eastern Quebec provided an opportunity for Canada Mortgage and Housing to obtain information about the cooling rates of homes that were without heat and occupants for several days. Local authorities were given permission to enter 31 residential homes in the Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu and measure the house air temperature and surface temperatures in the basement. House size and insulation levels were estimated and the date of construction was recorded. The mean temperature during the testing period was around -8 degrees C, with daytime highs of up to 3 degrees C. When a furnace stop operating in the winter, homeowners can expect the indoor air temperature to drop in the first several hours from 20 degrees C to 14 degrees C. However, from that point on, cooling occurs much more slowly because the temperature differential of indoor to outdoor air is now lower. The thermal mass of the house also plays a role in stabilizing the cooling rate. The concrete, gypsum, ceramics, wood, and furnishings take longer to lose their temperature. In addition, the earth around the basements has a high thermal mass which does not change temperatures readily. Incidental gains from the sun also increase the house temperature. This data should reassure homeowners who worry about leaving their homes in winter. Results show that in the event of a power failure, houses can take days or weeks to fall below freezing, depending on the severity of weather outside. 1 tab.  More>>
