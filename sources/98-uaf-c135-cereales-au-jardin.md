## Extraits intégraux

**Saison et choix**

> « Spring varieties are usually planted in early May and harvested in early September for a
> four-month growing season. These are the best-adapted varieties for Alaska's growing
> conditions. Winter varieties are usually planted in mid-August. [...] The grain is then harvested
> in mid-September for a 13-month growing season. [...] in Alaska they are susceptible to winter kill
> from desiccation, freezing of the rosette, and attacks of snowmold fungi [...] It is for these
> reasons that winter grains are not recommended. »

> « The outer fibrous hull on feed grains (feed barley and oats) is not digestible by non-ruminant
> animals or humans and must be removed in a de-hulling process. In hulless grains the hull is thin
> and papery, and detaches from the kernel during harvesting »

> « Barley is the most important grain crop grown in Alaska. It is well adapted to the long day
> length and short growing season found here. »

> « Oats are 7 to 10 days later in maturing than the early season barley varieties. Oats can be
> planted early in the season in areas that are poorly drained, as oat seed germinates well in cold
> and wet soils. Oats are also more tolerant of acidic soils than barley or wheat and can produce
> high yields when soil pH values range between 5.0 and 5.5. »

> « Because of the high fat content in oat grain, both dehulled and pearled oats can become rancid
> in about four days if the fat isn't first stabilized. [...] all dehulled and pearled oats need to
> be heated (kilning) in an oven at 240º F for 30 to 60 minutes. »

> « If the weather is warm and dry for a month after pollination then it [wheat] matures on average
> 10 days later than barley. If the weather for that same period is cool and wet then an additional
> 10 to 15 days are required to reach maturity. Light frosts during heading can cause sterility,
> while light frosts before the plant reaches full maturity stops any further grain development. »

> « Rye (Secale cereale L.) has been shown to be even less adapted for Alaska than wheat. Similar to
> wheat, rye has a long growing season requirement but yields less than many wheat varieties. Rye
> is susceptible to head shatter making it difficult to harvest »

**Rendement et semence**

> « Typical yields for hulless barley, hulless oats, hard red spring wheat, rye, and triticale run
> from 2,000–3,000 pounds per acre. [...] This translates to yields of approximately 5 to 7 pounds
> of clean ripe seed/100 sq. ft. (10 feet by 10 feet). This is a seed volume of about one gallon. If
> you save aside enough seed to plant another 100 sq. ft. garden plot next year you'll need about a
> quarter of a pound. »

> « If you decide to turn this grain into rolled flakes or whole grain flour then the yield will be
> 95 to 100 percent. »

> « Seeding rates for all small grains are around 90 to 100 pounds per acre of pure live seed. This
> is about a quarter of a pound for an area of 100 square feet (10 ft by 10 ft). [...] Small grains
> should be planted to a depth of 1.5 to 2.5 inches [...] The seed can be broadcast over the seedbed
> and raked in but this will often result in an uneven stand »

> « It is not uncommon to have hulless grain seed lots with less than 90 percent germination. »

> « Newly cleared soils are usually low in available nitrogen »

> « All small grains do well in soils that are slightly acidic to slightly alkaline, with a pH from
> 5.5 to 7.5. However, there will be better nutrient uptake in soils that have a pH range of 6.0 to
> 7.0. »

**Maladies, ergot, oiseaux**

> « Many different species of Fusarium fungi [...] can be easily identified by the pinkish color of
> the fungal mass on diseased kernels. [...] Fusarium molds can produce mycotoxins. »

> « Rye's biggest problem is the susceptibility to ergot disease (Claviceps purpurea). [...] Ergot can
> also infect other small grains with the following order of susceptibility: rye, triticale, barley,
> durum wheat, common wheat, and oats. »

> « [Gazelle rye] In Alaska, it is highly susceptible to ergot with average levels of contamination
> between 0.01 and 5.10 percent. »

> « Heavy crop damage can be inflicted by migratory waterfowl like cranes and geese in both the
> spring and fall. [...] they usually work the edges of gardens where they have a large field of
> vision. »

> « Because the hulls are loose on hulless varieties, any seedheads that lodge severely enough to
> touch the ground can quickly sprout while still in the head. »

**Récolte, battage, séchage**

> « All small grains stop growing when they reach the hard dough stage. The heads are light yellow in
> color and the kernels cannot be dented when pressed with a thumbnail. The typical moisture content
> of the grain at this stage is between 25 to 35 percent. [...] The grain must be around 13 to 15
> percent moisture for safe, long-term storage. »

> « Place the pie tin with the seed in your oven set at 140º to 150º F and allow the grain to dry
> for 12 hours or overnight. [...] The percent moisture of the grain = [(wet weight – dry
> weight)/wet weight] x 100. »

> « The plants can be cut with a hand scythe or a pair of pruning shears about three to four inches
> off of the ground [...] Place the cut plants head first into large paper or cloth sacks (never
> plastic) [...] the plants can be stacked in a bundle (or shock) [...] The excess moisture dries off
> of the plants and the grain in about 7 to 10 days after cutting in cool moist conditions and in 3
> to 4 days in hot dry weather. »

> « Head shatter, or loss of the grain due to physical contact, occurs on overripe grains [...]
> Six-row varieties are more susceptible to head shattering than two-row varieties. »

> « With the plants still in the sacks, place them on the floor (or any hard dry surface). Then
> simply start walking on the sacks at the ends where the heads are located. »

> « Too much heat can literally cook the seed, damaging the embryo and severely reducing the
> germination ability. »
