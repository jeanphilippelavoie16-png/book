# Pope (1923) — Pointes de chasse en acier, en verre ; essais de pénétration ; l'orignal à l'arc

- **Auteur :** Saxton T. Pope
- **Titre :** *Hunting with the Bow and Arrow*, G. P. Putnam's Sons, New York, 1923
- **Statut :** publié en 1923, **domaine public** aux États-Unis. Édition Project Gutenberg n° 8084.
- **URL :** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8084 (texte : https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8084/pg8084.txt)
- **Date de consultation :** 2026-09-24 (texte complet téléchargé dans le scratchpad de session,
  `pope1923.txt` ; les passages utiles sont recopiés ci-dessous mot à mot)
- **Langue :** anglais
- **Nature :** témoignage d'un chirurgien-archer qui a appris d'Ishi (Yahi) et chassé le gros
  gibier à l'arc, avec des essais de pénétration faits par lui-même. **Ni officiel, ni mesuré selon
  une méthode publiée** ; c'est la source ancienne la plus détaillée sur la fabrication d'une pointe
  large à la main.
- **Chapitre qui s'en sert :** 35b (§9, « La pointe large »)

## Extraits intégraux

### Les pointes d'Ishi (verre, obsidienne ; attache)

> « When ready for use, these heads were set on the end of the shaft with heated resin and bound in
> place with sinew which encircled the end of the arrow and crossed diagonally through the barb
> notches with many recurrences. »

> « Such a point has better cutting qualities in animal tissue than has steel. The latter is, of
> course, more durable. After entering civilization, Ishi preferred to use iron or steel blades of
> the same general shape, or having a short tang for insertion in the arrowhead. »

> « The little notches fashioned to hold the sinew binding below the barbs he shaped with a smaller
> piece of bone [...] »

> « Flint, plate glass, old bottle glass, onyx--all could be worked with equal facility. »

> « The general size of these points was two inches for length, seven-eighths for width, and
> one-eighth for thickness. »

> « He drilled this hole by fixing a long, sharp bone in the ground between his great toes and
> revolved the upright shaft between his palms on this fixed point, the buckskin binding keeping the
> wood from splitting. »

> « Its pull was about forty-five pounds [...] this seemed to be the ideal weight for hunting, and it
> certainly was adequate in his hands. » (l'arc d'Ishi)

### Force des arcs de chasse de Pope

> « The weight of a hunting bow should be from fifty to eighty pounds. One should start shooting with
> a bow not over fifty pounds, and preferably under that. [...] Our average bows pull seventy-five
> pounds. »

### Essais

> « Shooting a blunt arrow from a seventy-five pound bow at a white pine board an inch thick, the
> shaft will often go completely through it. A broad hunting head will penetrate two or three
> inches, then bind. But the broad-head will go through animal tissue better, even cutting bones in
> two [...] »

> « A box was so constructed that two opposite sides were formed by fresh deer skin tacked in place.
> The interior of the box was filled with bovine liver. [...] At a distance of ten yards I
> discharged an obsidian-pointed arrow and a steel-pointed arrow from a weak bow. [...] the
> steel-headed arrow uniformly penetrated a distance of twenty-two inches from the front surface of
> the box, while the obsidian uniformly penetrated thirty inches, or eight inches farther,
> approximately 25 per cent better penetration. »

> « In the same way we discovered that steel broad-heads sharpened by filing have a better
> meat-cutting edge than when ground on a stone. »

> « [...] it is not the exterior bleeding that kills the wounded animal so much as the internal
> hemorrhage. A sufficiently wide head on the arrow cuts a hole large enough to permit the escape of
> excess blood, and, as a matter of fact, nearly all of our shots are perforating, going completely
> through the body. »

> « Conical, blunt, and bodkin points lack the power of penetration in animal tissue inherent in
> broad-heads; correspondingly they do less damage. »

### Fabrication des pointes

> « The first is a blunt head made by binding the end of the shaft with thin tinned iron wire for
> half an inch and running on solder, then drilling a hole in the end of the shaft and inserting an
> inch round-headed screw. »

> « A second type of head we use is made of soft steel about a sixteenth of an inch thick. We cut it
> with a hack saw into a blunt, barbed, lanceolate shape having a blade about an inch long and half
> an inch wide, also a tang about the same length and three-eighths of an inch wide. This we set
> into a slot sawed in the arrow in the same plane as the nock, and bind the shaft with tinned wire
> [...] The end of the shaft has a gradual bevel where it meets the lateral face of the head. »

> « [...] we shoot a head whose blade is three inches long, an inch and a quarter wide, a trifle less
> than a thirty-second thick. [...] Its weight is half an ounce. The blades are made of spring
> steel. After annealing the steel we score it diagonally with a hack saw, when it may be broken in
> triangular pieces in a vise. With a cold chisel, an angular cut is made in the base to form the
> barbs. With a file and carborundum stone, they are edged and shaped into blades as sharp as
> knives. Soft, cold drawn steel will serve quite as well as spring steel for these blades, but it
> does not hold its edge. »

> « Be sure that your heads are straight and true. To set them on your shaft, cut the wood to fit,
> then heat a bit of ferrule cement and set them on in the same plane as the nock. [...] In the wilds
> we often fix the head with pine resin. Glue can be used, but it is not so good. »

### L'orignal (Art Young, Alaska, 1922-1925)

> « When he presented a fair target at seventy yards or so, Art drove an arrow at him. It struck deep
> in the flank, up to the feather ranging forward. The bull was only startled a trifle and trotted
> off a hundred yards. [...] soon had the satisfaction of seeing the moose waver in his course and
> lie down. »

> « Upon the second expedition a year later, Young bagged another moose. Here the arrow penetrated
> both sides of the chest and caused almost instant death [...] »

## Ce que le chapitre 35b en tire

- La pointe large coupe, la pointe ronde, conique ou en aiguille ne coupe pas et pénètre moins dans
  la chair (§9, la pointe large).
- Tôle d'acier de ressort recuite, rayée à la scie à métaux et cassée en triangles ; la base entaillée
  au ciseau à froid ; tranchant fait à la lime. **L'acier doux coupe aussi bien mais ne tient pas son
  fil.** Affûtée à la lime, elle coupe mieux la viande que polie à la pierre.
- Pose : soie dans une fente **sciée** au bout du fût, **dans le plan de l'encoche** ; bout du fût
  aminci en biseau vers la lame ; tenue à la résine de pin (« in the wilds »), ligature.
- Ligature du tendon croisée **dans des encoches taillées sous les ailettes** (Ishi).
- Ligaturer le bout du fût **avant** de le percer : la ligature l'empêche de fendre.
- Le verre de bouteille se taille comme la pierre ; mieux tranchant que l'acier, plus fragile.
- Ceux qui ont pris l'orignal à l'arc tiraient des arcs de **22,7 à 36,3 kg** ; la flèche qui a
  traversé la poitrine a tué presque sur-le-champ, celle du flanc a laissé la bête faire encore
  une centaine de verges.

## Ce que la source ne dit pas

- Rien sur un arc de bille non mesuré ; rien sur la tôle d'un baril ou d'une boîte de conserve.
- L'essai du foie n'a pas de méthode publiée (un seul tireur, un « weak bow » non chiffré).
- Les pointes de Pope sont soudées et rivées sur une douille de métal : le chapitre n'en garde que
  la lame, la soie et la ligature, faisables sans atelier.
