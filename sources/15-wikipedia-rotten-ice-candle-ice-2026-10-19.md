{{Short description|Melting or otherwise disintegrating ice on open water}}
[[File:Ice Melting on Lake Balaton.jpg|300px|thumb|Rotten ice melting on Lake Balaton]]
'''Rotten ice''' or '''candle ice''' is a loose term for [[ice]] that is melting or structurally disintegrating due to being honeycombed<ref name=":0">{{cite encyclopedia|title=rotten ice|encyclopedia=Glossary of Meteorology|publisher=American Meteorological Society|url=http://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Rotten_ice}}</ref> by liquid water, air, or contaminants trapped between the initial growth of ice crystals. It may appear transparent or splotchy grey, and it is generally found after spring or summer thaws, presenting a danger to those traveling or spending time in outdoor recreation. The increase of rotten ice vs. solid ice in the Arctic affects ocean-atmosphere heat transfer and year-to-year ice formation, as well as the lives of the [[Inuit]], sea mammals such as [[walrus]] and [[polar bear]], and the microorganisms that live inside the ice. 

Rotten ice has a subtype called "candle ice", which has a columnar structure. Like other rotten ice, it poses a hazard to humans due to its lack of structure.

== Properties and life cycle ==
Compared to solid ice, rotten ice has "high [[porosity]] and enhanced permeability."<ref name=":1">{{Cite journal|last1=Frantz|first1=Carie M.|last2=Light|first2=Bonnie|last3=Farley|first3=Samuel M.|last4=Carpenter|first4=Shelly|last5=Lieblappen|first5=Ross|last6=Courville|first6=Zoe|last7=Orellana|first7=Mónica V.|last8=Junge|first8=Karen|date=2019-03-05|title=Physical and optical characteristics of heavily melted "rotten" Arctic sea ice|url=https://tc.copernicus.org/articles/13/775/2019/|journal=The Cryosphere|language=English|volume=13|issue=3|pages=775–793|doi=10.5194/tc-13-775-2019|bibcode=2019TCry...13..775F |issn=1994-0416|doi-access=free}}</ref> This porosity facilitates "large convective transport of nutrients, salt and heat at the onset of fall freeze-up", which  [[algal bloom]] may also contribute.<ref name=":6">{{Cite journal|last1=Haas|first1=Christian|last2=Thomas|first2=David N.|last3=Bareiss|first3=Jörg|date=2001|title=Surface properties and processes of perennial Antarctic sea ice in summer|journal=Journal of Glaciology|language=en|volume=47|issue=159|pages=613–625|doi=10.3189/172756501781831864|bibcode=2001JGlac..47..613H |issn=0022-1430|doi-access=free}}</ref> It forms on open water when [[snowpack]] and ice are mixed together{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}} or [[Sea ice|when polar ice melts]] during the spring<ref name=":2">{{Cite web|last=Alberta and Northwest Territories Branch|title=Ice Safety Tips|url=https://www.lifesaving.org/public-education/water-smart-tips2/ice-safety-tips|access-date=2021-03-18|website=Lifesaving Society}}</ref> or summer.<ref name=":3">{{Cite magazine|last=Ehrlich|first=Gretel|date=2015-04-01|title=[Letter from Greenland] {{!}} Rotten Ice|url=https://harpers.org/archive/2015/04/rotten-ice/|access-date=2021-03-18|magazine=Harper's Magazine|language=en}}</ref> If saturated with water, rotten ice may look dark or transparent, similar to new [[Black ice (lakes)|black ice]],<ref name=":0" /> but otherwise it may look grey and splotchy.<ref name=":5">{{Cite web|title=Ice in lakes and rivers - Ice decay|url=https://www.britannica.com/science/lake-ice|access-date=2021-03-18|website=Encyclopedia Britannica|language=en}}</ref> Though rotten ice may appear strong, it is weak—even several feet thick may not hold a person's weight. On land, it is difficult or impossible to climb.{{Citation needed|date=March 2021}}

Rotting may begin at the top or bottom surface and occurs due to absorption of heat from the sun.<ref name=":5" /> In general, ice melting may accelerate due to various factors. Water from underneath the ice can erode the ice and cause it to be thinner without a sign on the surface. Runoff from upstream melting, roads (especially salted), and snow can weaken the ice, and "tree stumps, rocks and docks absorb heat from the sun, causing ice around them to melt."<ref name=":4">{{Cite web|last=Community|date=2012-04-01|title=Beware of 'rotten' ice|url=https://www.terracestandard.com/community/beware-of-rotten-ice/|access-date=2021-03-18|website=Terrace Standard|language=en-US}}</ref> Ice may melt faster along shorelines. Ice under a layer of snow will be thinner and weaker due to the snow's insulating effect; a new snowfall can also warm up and melt existing ice.<ref name=":4" /> However, snow or snow ice may also absorb or reflect incoming solar radiation and prevent rotting until the snow is melted.<ref name=":5" /> Regardless of thickness, ice will be weakened by multiple freezes and thaws or layers of snow inside the ice itself.<ref name=":4" /> It melts more quickly than solid ice.<ref name=":7">{{Cite web|last1=Strauss|first1=Ben|last2=Central|first2=Climate|date=2010-06-23|title=NASA: May's Melting of Arctic Ice Close to Speed of July|url=https://insideclimatenews.org/news/23062010/nasa-mays-melting-arctic-ice-close-speed-july/|access-date=2021-03-18|website=Inside Climate News|language=en-US}}</ref>

Certain types of bacteria in rotten ice pores produce polymer-like substances, which may influence the physical properties of the ice. A team from the [[University of Washington]] studying this phenomenon hypothesizes that the polymers may provide a stabilizing effect to the ice.<ref>{{Cite web|title=Extreme Summer Melt|url=https://apl.uw.edu/project/project.php?id=rotten_ice|access-date=2021-03-18|website=Applied Physics Laboratory at the University of Washington}}</ref> However, other scientists have found algae and other microorganisms produce pigments or help create a substance, [[cryoconite]], all of which increase rotting and further the growth of the microorganisms.<ref name=":6" /><ref>{{Cite web|last=Pfeifer|first=Hazel|date=2021-01-20|title=Microscopic life is melting Greenland's ice sheet|url=https://www.cnn.com/2020/12/14/world/microscopic-life-melting-greenland-ice-sheet-c2e-spc-intl/index.html|access-date=2021-03-18|website=CNN}}</ref> 

== Role in climate science ==
In 2009, researchers studying the [[Beaufort Sea]] north of Alaska found that most of the ice present had become rotten ice, instead of thick, solid ice that had either been newly formed or present for multiple years. This [[Arctic sea ice decline|decline in multiyear ice]] contradicted previous impressions that Arctic ice was recovering from [[climate change]] and "had implications for climate science and marine vessel transport in the Arctic."<ref>{{cite news|last=American Geophysical Union|date=21 January 2010|title=Is ice 'rotten' in the Beaufort Sea|newspaper=Science Daily|url=https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/01/100121164011.htm}}</ref> Other research has found that the increased permeability of rotten ice can "contribute to ocean–atmosphere heat transfer."<ref name=":1" /> Future increases of rotten ice matter influence long-term ice cover: "If the ice melts completely, then the open ocean will form new ice in the autumn. Only ice remaining at the end of summer can become second-year and subsequently multiyear ice."<ref name=":1" /> As rotten ice exposes more of the ocean, it also creates a feedback loop where the exposed darker ocean absorbs more heat, which melts more ice and exposes more ocean.<ref name=":7" />

In the years leading up to 2015, [[Greenland]]'s ice cover has decreased to "a rotten ice regime", with months of solid ice decreasing from 9 per year to 2–3, and with thickness decreasing from {{Convert|6-10|ft}} to {{Convert|7|in|cm}} by 2004.<ref name=":3" /> The decline of solid land ice to rotten ice strongly disrupts travel and subsistence hunting for the [[Greenlandic Inuit|local Inuit]], as well as travel and habitat for sea mammals.<ref name=":3" /> In the future, the shedding rotted or melted ice may affect coastlines of other continents via [[sea level rise|rising sea levels]].<ref name=":7" />[[File:Candle Ice (5877950711).jpg|thumb|300x300px|Candle ice in Canada]]

== Candle ice ==
Candle ice (sometimes known as [[needle ice]])<ref>{{Cite book|last=Office|first=United States Hydrographic|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=MX1Ljv4bv3UC&dq=%22candle+ice%22&pg=PA78|title=Sailing Directions for Northern U.S.S.R.: Mys Kanin Nos to Ostron Dikson|date=1954|pages=78|language=en}}</ref> is a form of rotten ice that develops in columns perpendicular to the surface of a [[lake]] or other body of water.<ref>{{Cite encyclopedia|title=Candle ice|encyclopedia=Glossary of Meteorology|publisher=[[American Meteorological Society]]|url=https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/Candle_ice|access-date=2021-03-17|date=2012-02-20}}</ref> It makes a clinking sound when the "candles" are broken apart and floating in the water, bumping up against each other.<ref>{{Cite book|author1=Bailey, William H.|title=The surface climates of Canada|author2=Oke, T. R.|author3=Rouse, Wayne R.|publisher=McGill-Queen's University Press|year=1997|isbn=978-0-7735-1672-4|location=Montreal}}</ref> As ice from a larger surface melts, the formation of candle ice "progressively increases with time, temperature, and quantity of water melt runoff."<ref>{{Cite book|last=Swinzow|first=George K.|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=AP5UetOOiQIC&dq=%22candle+ice%22&pg=PA27|title=Ice Cover of an Arctic Proglacial Lake|publisher=U.S. Army Materiel Command, Cold Regions Research & Engineering Laboratory|year=1966|pages=27|language=en}}</ref> This occurs due to the hexagonal structure of the ice crystals; minerals such as salt, as well as other contaminants, can be trapped between the crystals when they initially form, and melting will begin at these boundaries due to the trapped contaminants.<ref>{{Cite book|last=[[United States Army Corps of Engineers]]|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=GFBhnDGTVtQC&q=candle+ice|title=Ice Engineering|date=2002|publisher=University Press of the Pacific|isbn=978-0-89875-844-3|location=Honolulu, HI|pages=2–1|language=en}}</ref> No matter the thickness,<ref name=":2" /> it can be dangerous due to its lack of horizontal structure, which means there will be no rim to grab for any person who falls through.<ref>{{Cite book|last=Tawrell|first=Paul|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=deRKF5kv5wwC&dq=%22candle+ice%22&pg=PA305|title=Camping & Wilderness Survival: The Ultimate Outdoors Book|date=2006|publisher=Paul Tawrell|isbn=978-0-9740820-2-8|pages=305|language=en}}</ref>
