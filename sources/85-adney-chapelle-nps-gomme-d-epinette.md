### Extraits intégraux (anglais, tels qu'imprimés)

> « The spruce gum was gathered and tempered. […] As the spruce gum melted easily, great
> temperature was not required. […] Another method was to boil water in a bark container and drop
> in the spruce gum, which melted and floated on top of the water in such a consistency that it
> could be skimmed off with a bark spoon or dipper. Chips and dirt were skimmed off the hot gum
> with a strip of bark or a flat stick. »

> « Tempering, done after the gum was melted, consisted of adding animal fat and a little finely
> powdered charcoal. The mixture was then tested by dipping a strip of bark into it and then into
> cold water. The strip was bent to see if it cracked the spruce gum; if it did, too much
> tempering material had been added and more gum was required. If no cracking occurred, the gum on
> the strip was held in the hand for a few moments to see if it became tacky or could be rubbed
> off the strip; if either occurred, more tempering was needed. The method of tempering had many
> variations. One was to remelt the gum a number of times; this darkened it and made it harder. »

> « Instead of spruce gum, in some areas, pine resin was used, tempered with tallow and sometimes
> charcoal. The Indians in the East sometimes used remelted spruce gum to which a little tallow had
> been added, making a light brown or almost transparent mixture. »

> « For repair work, when melted spruce gum could not be procured in the usual manner, hard
> globules and flakes of gum scraped from a fallen spruce tree were used. These could not be
> easily melted, so they were first chewed thoroughly until soft; then the gum was spread over a
> seam. This type of gum would not stick well unless it were smoothed with a glowing stick, and
> hence was used only in emergencies. »

> « The Malecites used only spruce gum tempered with animal fat. The gum, heated until it is
> sufficiently soft to pour like heavy syrup, is spread with a small wooden paddle or spoon, and is
> then worked into the seam and smoothed by rubbing with the thumb dipped in water to prevent the
> gum from sticking and burning. »

## Ce que le chapitre 85 en tire

- **Les trois ingrédients du chapitre sont confirmés** : gomme d'épinette, gras animal (suif, graisse
  d'ours), charbon pilé fin — « a little » charbon.
- **Aucune proportion n'est publiée** : le mélange se règle à l'essai, comme le chapitre le dit.
- **L'essai de la source est plus précis que « une goutte refroidie »** : une languette d'écorce
  trempée dans le mélange, puis dans l'eau froide, puis **pliée** (la gomme ne doit pas fendre),
  puis **tenue quelques instants dans la main** (la gomme ne doit ni devenir collante ni s'enlever
  au frottement).
- **La gomme se nettoie à l'eau bouillante** : elle fond et flotte, on l'écume ; copeaux et saletés
  s'écument aussi.
- **En dépannage**, la gomme dure ramassée sur un arbre tombé se mâche jusqu'à ce qu'elle
  ramollisse, s'étale sur la couture et se lisse au tison.
- **Refondre plusieurs fois** fonce la gomme et la durcit.

## Attention à la lecture

Le sens de la correction, tel qu'imprimé, va à l'inverse de l'intuition : la source écrit que si la
gomme **fend**, il y a **trop** de matière d'adoucissement (« tempering material », gras **et**
charbon) et qu'il faut plus de gomme ; si elle **colle** ou s'enlève, il en faut **plus**. Le
passage est cité tel quel ; la fiche ne le corrige pas. Le chapitre publie l'essai (fend / colle)
sans le sens de la correction, qu'il tient de son propre mécanisme.
