Slitted and Floating Row Covers

Row covers function to enhance growth and yield by modifying the temperatures around plants in the spring and fall, or in combination with low tunnels, during the winter. They are also used for frost, hail, and wind protection, and to exclude certain pests. There are two general types: slitted or perforated polyethylene, and spun-bonded fabric. In addition, fine-mesh insect netting is now commonly used in vegetable production for pest exclusion; unlike spun-bonded row cover, insect netting provides little or no frost protection and much less heat retention. Heavier weight row covers can provide several degrees of frost protection, while lightweight "non-heating" or summer weight covers offer less heat enhancement and can be used in summer for insect protection. These materials can be used with or without hoops ("floating row cover") depending on their weight and the fragility of the crop underneath. Newer types of knitted or woven lightweight row cover or insect netting (e.g., 'ProtekNet' by Agrinovations) are available; they can be used with or without hoops, are quite durable, and will exclude insects. 

Row covers are installed right after planting or seeding and are left covering the crop for several weeks, depending on crop type and season. For fruiting crops and cucurbits, covers can be left in place for approximately 3–5 weeks until pollination is needed or the crop outgrows the space under the cover. Other crops that are low-growing and do not require pollination can remain under cover as long as the temperature benefit is useful. Sweet corn may be left covered with spun-bonded row cover until pretassel stage. If the crop is pressing against the cover, either loosen or remove it. Row cover removal timing is more critical in some crops, such as tomato and pepper, as they cannot tolerate extremely high temperatures that might develop under the covers (especially polyethylene). Covers must be removed for crops requiring insect or wind pollination.

A unique system using clear plastic has been developed for sweet corn, allowing earlier planting, improved germination, and maturity 5–12 days earlier. The corn is planted in two rows 16" apart, with seeds in shallow trenches and soil mounded between rows. Plastic is removed when plants reach 6–12 inches tall or sooner if temperatures under the plastic exceed 100°F, typically at 80–85°F ambient temperature. Floating fabric row covers can also be used successfully for longer periods, and are particularly effective for "super sweet" or "shrunken-2" corn varieties.

Slitted or perforated row covers are clear polyethylene films with slits cut or holes drilled to provide ventilation when the plastic loosens under hot conditions. Under cool conditions, the plastic is taut and the slits remain closed. Very little water condensation occurs under perforated plastic covers. There is generally less frost protection under slitted or perforated row covers than under a solid cover. Compared with spun-bonded row covers, clear polyethylene low tunnels provide greater daytime heating.

Plastic row covers will require support with wire hoops. A piece of No. 9 wire cut about 65" long makes a hoop that is about 3' wide at the base and 14" tall in the center of the row after inserting each leg of the hoop in the soil. Edges of the cover should be secured with soil.

In diversified vegetable and/or berry operations, row covers can be a cost effective and convenient tool for producing early, high quality crops. Edges are usually held down with soil, soil-filled bags, boards, sandbags, or rocks. Row covers provide sufficient growth enhancement by raising air temperatures during the day and moderating cold temperatures at night. Spun-bonded materials also allow rain and overhead irrigation to pass through. The result is earlier harvests, and in some cases, higher total yields. While lightweight row covers do not provide reliable frost protection, they may be helpful when temperatures drop 2–3ºF below freezing. Heavyweight covers can provide more frost protection, but they block much of the sunlight, resulting in slower growth. A key benefit of row covers is that, if they are sealed along the edges, they exclude a wide range of insect pests.

A frequent mistake made when using row cover is allowing excessive heat buildup. Typically, if outside air temperatures reach around 80°F, it's advisable to consider providing additional ventilation. During unseasonably warm years, covers may end up causing more harm than good and should be removed shortly after application. When excess heat is a concern but insect exclusion is still needed, insect netting is often a better choice than spun-bonded row cover.

Which Types to Use? There are several different weights, measured in ounces per square yard, ounces per square foot, or grams per square meter.

  • Lightweight: 0.45 oz/yd2, allow 90% light transmission, mainly for insect control. Also used in heat-sensitive crops as it provides only a minimal heat increase during the day. Less expensive than heavier materials, but more likely to rip from wind and sharp objects (fingernails, boots, deer hooves, stakes, etc.). They last two seasons with careful handling.
  • Medium weight: 0.5–1.0 oz/yd2, allow 70%–85% light transmission and give 4–6°F for frost protection, for spring, fall, and overwintering crops. 
  • Heavyweight: 1.5–2.2 oz/yd2, allow 30%–50% light transmission and give up to 8°F of frost protection for overwintering crops. They are durable enough to last for several seasons when handled with care. 

Support and Fastening. Many crops can handle floating covers without any support, including lettuce, greens, crucifers, onions, potatoes, strawberries, and sweet corn. Those with tender, exposed growing points (tomatoes, peppers, and cucurbit crops) should have some support to prevent damage from wind abrasion. Wire hoops or short stakes with a smooth top to prevent tearing placed at 3- to 6-foot intervals provide good support. Secure the edges of the cover with soil, soil-filled plastic bags, or metal or plastic pins or staples. For holding the cover in place, soil is the most secure in high winds, but the edges are difficult to unearth after repeated wetting and drying, while soil bags make it easier to lift or move covers and prolong the life of the material.

Sizes. Row covers can be purchased in 3–60' widths and 20–2,250' lengths. Wider covers are more labor efficient because they have less edge to bury per covered area, but don't try to lay them in a strong wind!

Weed Control. Row covers provide a good environment for weeds too; watch for weed growth under row covers. Covers can be rolled to the edge of the bed for cultivation or herbicide application, and then replaced.

Storage. Row covers should be stored away from direct sunlight as soon as they are removed from the field. While many have been treated to reduce UV degradation, they will last longer if unnecessary UV exposure is prevented. Fold or roll covers in a systematic way so they can be carefully unfolded for next year's use. 

Insect Control. Some insects overwinter in the soil where the crop was grown, and emerge the following spring. In such cases, only use row covers on rotated fields. Also, seal the edges of the cover immediately after installation. If the cover is removed for cultivation, it should be done when insects are less active, such as on a cloudy day or in the morning. Scout under row covers regularly for trapped pests, weeds, disease, and excess heat.
