# Arbustes fruitiers rustiques : camerise, amélanchier, pommetier, argousier, gadellier et groseillier

Six pages, trois organismes publics canadiens (Université de la Saskatchewan, gouvernement du
Manitoba, gouvernement de l'Ontario). Toutes consultées le **27 septembre 2026**. Elles servent au
chapitre 99, §4, §5-D, §5-E, §6, §7 et §9.

## 1. Camerise (*Lonicera caerulea*) — Université de la Saskatchewan

- Bors, B., « Growing Haskap in Canada », USask Fruit Program —
  https://research-groups.usask.ca/fruit/documents/haskap/growinghaskapinCanada.pdf —
  `99-usask-bors-growing-haskap.pdf` (+ `-texte.txt`)
- « Haskaps », Gardening at USask —
  https://gardening.usask.ca/gardening-advice/gardenline-nested-pages/food-plant-pages/fruit/haskap.php —
  `99-usask-camerise.html`

> « The plant has few pests and is the first fruit crop to ripen each season (earlier than
> strawberries by a few weeks). The plant is well behaved: it doesn't sucker, has no thorns, needs
> little pruning in early years and tends to fruit when very young. »

> « Lonicera caerulea is a circumpolar species native to northern boreal forests in Asia, Europe, and
> North America. It is mainly found in low lying wet areas or high in mountains. »

> « Hardiness: The plants are extremely hardy. We have never seen winter damage on them. [...] Our
> worst winter had a low -47C; no problem. »

> « Within-row spacing is recommended at 1 meter if you want the plants to grow into a hedge. At 1.3
> meter they would probably remain as individual bushes. »

> « In Saskatchewan haskap bloom from late April to early May and can take -7C to an open flower
> without damage. Haskap needs two unrelated varieties in close proximity for good pollination. »

> « Russian researchers recommend a pH between 5 and 7. »

> « Windbreaks: Protection to the west and north of any prairie orchard is highly recommended. Winter
> damage is often a function of desiccation caused by direct exposure to prevailing winds. »

> « Birds, particularly Cedar Waxwings, love Haskap. »

> « Pruning should be undertaken in late winter or early spring. You should mainly thin out older
> branches when the bush gets too dense. Never remove more that 25% of a bush in any year. »

> « If some unfortunate event occurs that kills the top of the bushes [...] it is quite possible the
> plant will come back from the crown. They are on their own roots so the regrowth will be the same
> variety. »

> « Bushes often bear a few fruit the year after planting but it will be 3 or 4 years before the
> bushes are big enough to get a few kilos per bush. »

> « Don't be too anxious: bite some berries in half, if they are green inside they aren't ready. »

> « Our oldest plants were bearing about 7 kilos / bush after 5 years. In Japan they expect their
> better plants to produce 3 kilos »

> « We have some ornamental blue honeysuckles at the University that are over 30 years old. »

> (page USask) « A common misconception is that one plant acts as a 'pollinator' for the other and
> doesn't produce fruit. This is false. Both plants are capable of producing fruit. » ; « They are
> hardy to Zones 1 - 4. »

## 2. Amélanchier (*Amelanchier alnifolia*, « saskatoon ») — Université de la Saskatchewan

- https://gardening.usask.ca/gardening-advice/gardenline-nested-pages/food-plant-pages/fruit/saskatoon.php —
  `99-usask-amelanchier.html`

> « Saskatoons are hardy to Zone 1 and they can survive temperatures up to -60C »

> « As the blooms appear early in the season, ensure they are not planted in a frost pocket.
> Saskatoons are susceptible to saskatoon-juniper rust so do your best to plant them at least 2 km
> away from any native junipers to avoid this disease. »

> « Saskatoons should be planted about 2.5 - 5 cm (1" - 2") deeper than they were in the nursery
> container as this will encourage a multi-stemmed shrub rather than suckers »

> « They should be spaced at least 1 - 1.5 m (3' - 5') apart from each other. When planted in a
> hedgerow, they will fill in the space between each plant within five years to form a solid hedge. »

> « Saskatoons are self fruitful so only one plant is required however saskatoons that have been
> cross pollinated by bees will produce more fruit. »

> « saskatoons have a very shallow root system that does not compete well with other plants »

> « Once the plant gets old and unproductive, it can be chopped down about 15 cm (6") above the soil
> surface and allowed to re-grow. This will rejuvenate the plant. »

> « their bark is a preferred food source for deer and rabbits so protecting the trees from wildlife
> in rural areas is a must. »

> « Saskatoons can be grown from seeds, suckers, hard or soft wood cuttings and tissue culture. »

> « Wild saskatoon bushes are all genetic individuals so each will have their own unique flavour and
> qualities. If you're using a wild shrub for woody propagation, pick the best tasting shrub you can
> find since the "new" plant will taste similarly »

> « In late winter or very early spring, place pruned branches in a bucket of water which will
> stimulate growth of roots to grow. The branches can be planted out in spring when the roots are
> well developed. »

> « Saskatoons do not ripen evenly so multiple pickings are required. »

## 3. Pommiers et pommetiers (*Malus*) — Université de la Saskatchewan

- https://gardening.usask.ca/gardening-advice/gardenline-nested-pages/food-plant-pages/fruit/apples.php —
  `99-usask-pommiers.html`

> « Apples are self-incompatible so they require another tree of a different variety to provide the
> pollen and get fruit. »

> « Apples and crabapples can pollinate each other because they are the same species. The only
> exception is certain ornamental double-flowering crabapples which do not produce pollen »

> « Note that two apple trees of different varieties separated by a maximum distance of 100 feet are
> needed for cross-pollination. »

> « We do not recommend saving seeds from apple trees. Apple tree seeds do not breed true: the apple
> that is produced will not be the same as the tree you collected it from. »

> « Prairie-hardy apple varieties are grafted on to a root stock [...] if Siberian crab-apple
> rootstock is used the mature height will be about 6 - 9 m (20'-30') feet. »

> « A newly planted apple or crab apple can take up to two years to establish and may take as long
> as 5 years to begin producing fruit. »

> « Home gardeners on the prairies and far north need to pay attention to the ripening dates as some
> years the fall is not long enough to harvest an apple that ripens in October. »

> (essais du Yukon) « Ultra Hardy — Noret, Trailman, Rescue — Should survive and fruit most years
> without winter protection in sheltered sites like downtown Whitehorse. Wrapping for winter is
> recommended in colder, more exposed locations. »

> « Apple trees usually require winter protection and should be pruned in multi-stemmed shrub form
> to enhance hardiness and reduce winterkill. »

> (tableau) « Rescue — 3.5 cm — crab apple — early — stores for 3 weeks » ; « Dolgo — 1.5 cm — crab
> apple — mid — excellent for making jelly »

## 4. Argousier (*Hippophae rhamnoides*) — Gouvernement du Manitoba

- « Sea Buckthorn Production in Manitoba » —
  https://www.gov.mb.ca/agriculture/crops/crop-management/fruit-crops/sea-buckthorn-production.html —
  `99-manitoba-argousier.html`

> « The trees are dioecious meaning there are male and female plants. [...] Very high vitamin C and
> anti-oxidant properties. »

> « Only female plants produce fruit. Unfortunately, plant type cannot be determined until flowers are
> produced. Flowers are small, yellow and appear before the leaves being produced on three-year-old
> wood. Sea buckthorn plants are very hardy and can withstand temperatures of -45 to 103 °F (-43 to
> 40 °C). To ensure adequate pollination and suitable numbers of female trees, it is necessary for
> the orchard to have between seven and 12% of the trees as pollinators. »

> « Sea buckthorn can be grown in very poor soils including river banks, steep slopes, and acid and
> alkaline soils. Sea buckthorn is also salt tolerant. Sea buckthorn grows on soils with a pH of 5.5
> to 8.3 [...] Plants should be spaced three feet (1 m) between plants [...] Sea buckthorn is a
> nitrogen fixing plant. »

> « The fruit is orange when ripe at the end of August/beginning of September and is very difficult
> harvest. Although the fruit is ripe, it is not easily removed from the tree. [...] Other techniques
> of removing the branch from the tree work well but it is still very labour intensive. Expected
> yields are around 11 to 15 lb. (5 to 7 kg) per plant »

## 5. Gadelliers, cassissiers, groseilliers (*Ribes*) — Gouvernement de l'Ontario

- « Growing currants and gooseberries in home gardens » —
  https://www.ontario.ca/page/growing-currants-and-gooseberries-home-gardens —
  `99-ontario-gadelliers-groseilliers.html`

> « Plantings should remain productive for at least 8–10 years »

> « Plant gooseberries and red currants 1.0–1.25 m apart in rows. Black currants are more vigorous
> and should be spaced about 0.6–1.5 m apart. »

> « Set the plants slightly deeper than they were growing in the nursery [...] Prune branches to a
> length of 10–15 cm at planting time. »

> « Remove any blossoms that appear the year the plants are set. »

> « Plants need about 25 mm of water each week from bloom time to the end of harvest. »

> « Black currants produce the best fruit on 1-year-old wood. [...] Keep a total of 10–12 shoots per
> mature bush, with about half being 1-year-old shoots. [...] Remove all shoots that are more than 3
> years old. Make pruning cuts close to the ground. »

> « Red currants and gooseberries produce most of their fruit on spurs that are located on 2- and
> 3-year-old wood. After pruning, a healthy bush should have 3–4 shoots each of 1-, 2- and 3-year-old
> shoots (a total of 9–12 shoots). Remove all shoots older than 3 years. »

> « Currants and gooseberries bloom early in the spring. Severe frosts can injure blossoms and young
> developing berries. Frosts cause fewer problems in sites with good air drainage. In small plantings,
> cloth or paper covers can be put over plants for frost protection. Plastic usually gives little or
> no protection. »

> « Currant and gooseberry cultivars are self-fruitful and do not need pollen from another cultivar
> to produce good crops. »

> « Plants should not be permitted to bear fruit the year they are planted. In the second year, a
> light crop can be harvested; and, by the third year, plants usually bear full crops. Beginning in
> the third year, a harvest of 1 kg per plant is considered average, However, yields of black
> currants are often only about half of this amount. »

> « Currants and American-type gooseberry cultivars can be propagated from cuttings. Take cuttings in
> late fall from healthy wood produced that summer. Make cuttings 15–20 cm long with the bottom cut
> just below a bud, and the top cut about 10 mm above a bud. That fall, set cuttings 15 cm apart in
> well-drained soil in a nursery area. Plant them deep enough so that one or two buds extend out of
> the soil and cover them with straw. [...] Cuttings can also be taken in early spring before buds
> leaf out. [...] After a season's growth, plants grown from cuttings can be planted in their
> permanent place. »

> « European types can be propagated by layering in fall or spring. Bend down branches, still
> attached to the plants, and partly cover them with soil. Use pegs to hold down the branches. Roots
> will form along the branches where they contact the soil. After a season's growth, the branches
> should have rooted well enough for digging. »

> « The fungus causing this disease spends part of its life cycle on currants or gooseberries and
> part on white pine. The disease can cause serious problems with white (five-needle) pines. Do not
> plant currants or gooseberries within 300 m of susceptible pines. Black currant cultivars such as
> Titania and Consort are resistant to rust. »

> « Black currants are prized for their distinctive flavour [...] They are also rich in Vitamin C. »

## Ce que le livre ne tire pas de ces pages

Les noms de cultivars ne s'impriment pas, sauf deux pommetiers (« Rescue », « Dolgo ») et aucun
camerisier nommé : un nom de cultivar vaut ce que vaut la pépinière qui le vend, et le chapitre dit
plutôt comment choisir (deux variétés non apparentées qui fleurissent ensemble). Les engrais
chimiques, l'irrigation goutte-à-goutte, les herbicides et les filets d'oiseaux du commerce ne
s'impriment pas. Les zones de rusticité ne s'impriment pas : voir `99-NON-OBTENUES.md` §2.
